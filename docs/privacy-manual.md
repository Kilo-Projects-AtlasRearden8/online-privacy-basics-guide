**A beginner's manual for personal digital privacy**

## About this guide

This manual walks you through five everyday privacy topics, one step at a time:

1. **Passwords** — strong, unique passwords and a password manager
2. **Two-factor authentication** — a second lock on your accounts
3. **Data-broker opt-outs** — removing your info from people-search sites
4. **Private browsing** — browsing without saving history on your device
5. **Phone and email hygiene** — keeping your number and inbox cleaner

Each section ends with an **action checklist** you can work through at your own
pace. Do one, some, or all — whatever feels right for you.

This guide is for education. It is not legal or professional security advice.

## 1. Passwords

### Why passwords matter

A password is the key to your account. If someone guesses it or steals it from
a leak, they can get in. Two things keep passwords safe:

- **Length** — longer is stronger
- **Uniqueness** — never reuse the same password on more than one site

### What to do

**Use a password manager.** A password manager creates and stores strong
passwords for you, so you only need to remember one main password. Built-in
managers (Apple iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager) work well for most
people.

**Make your main password long and memorable.** Think of a sentence or a few
unrelated words strung together, like `correct-horse-battery-staple-blue`. Long
and silly is better than short and weird.

**Never reuse passwords.** If one site leaks, attackers try that same password
everywhere. A unique password per site stops that chain reaction.

**Don't write passwords on paper or email them to yourself.** If you must write
one down, keep the paper somewhere safe at home — never in your wallet.

**Don't share your password.** If someone asks for your password "to help," say
no. Real support teams will never ask for your password.

### Password checklist

- [ ] Install or turn on a password manager (e.g., Apple Passwords, Google Password Manager, Bitwarden)
- [ ] Change your most important accounts first (email, bank, phone carrier)
- [ ] Give each account its own unique password
- [ ] Make your manager's main password long and memorable
- [ ] Save your manager's recovery code somewhere safe (printed, at home)
- [ ] Never reuse a password on two different sites

## 2. Two-factor authentication (2FA)

### What 2FA is

Two-factor authentication adds a second step after your password. Even if
someone has your password, they still can't get in without the second factor —
usually a code from your phone or an app.

### What to do

**Turn on 2FA where it matters most.** Start with your email, your phone
carrier, and your bank. Email is the most important because attackers use it to
reset passwords everywhere else.

**Pick the right method.** An authenticator app (Google Authenticator, Microsoft
Authenticator, Authy) is better than text messages (SMS). SMS can be intercepted
in rare cases. Use SMS if it's your only option — it's still much better than
nothing.

**Save backup codes.** When you turn on 2FA, the site gives you backup codes.
Print them or save them somewhere safe. If you lose your phone, backup codes let
you back in.

### 2FA checklist

- [ ] Turn on 2FA for your email account
- [ ] Turn on 2FA for your phone carrier account
- [ ] Turn on 2FA for your bank and other money accounts
- [ ] Prefer an authenticator app over text messages when available
- [ ] Save backup codes somewhere safe (printed, at home)
- [ ] Test that 2FA works (log out and log back in once)

## 3. Data-broker opt-outs

### What data brokers are

Data brokers are companies that collect and sell personal information — names,
addresses, phone numbers, relatives, and more. People-search sites let anyone
look you up. Opting out removes your listing from many of these sites.

### What to do

**Start with the biggest sites.** Common ones include Whitepages, Spokeo,
MyLife, Intelius, and BeenVerified. Each has an opt-out page, usually linked at
the bottom of their site under "Opt out," "Privacy," or "Do not sell my info."

**Expect to repeat yourself.** Most sites make you verify by email or phone,
and listings can come back over time. Check again every 6–12 months.

**Use a removal service if you want help.** Paid services (e.g., DeleteMe,
Optery, Incogni) contact brokers for you. They cost money but save time. Free
DIY opt-outs work too — they just take longer.

**Be realistic.** Opt-outs reduce what's easy to find, but they can't erase
everything. Government records and public filings stay public.

### Data-broker checklist

- [ ] Search your name + city on a few people-search sites to see what's listed
- [ ] Opt out of Whitepages, Spokeo, MyLife, Intelius, and BeenVerified
- [ ] Save confirmation emails from each opt-out
- [ ] Check again in 6–12 months to see if listings returned
- [ ] Consider a paid removal service if DIY feels like too much
- [ ] Don't panic if some listings remain — opt-outs reduce, not eliminate

## 4. Private browsing

### What private browsing does

Private browsing (Incognito in Chrome, Private in Safari/Firefox) stops your
browser from saving history, cookies, and form entries on your device after you
close the window. It does **not** make you invisible online — websites, your
employer, school, or internet provider can still see your activity.

### What to do

**Use private browsing on shared or public devices.** Library computers, a
friend's tablet, or any device you don't own: open a private window so you
don't leave passwords or history behind.

**Use it for one-off searches.** Shopping for a gift, looking up a medical
topic, or checking prices without cookies affecting results — private windows
keep those searches off your device history.

**Close the window when done.** Private browsing only protects you while the
window is open. Close it when you're finished.

**Remember the limits.** Private browsing doesn't hide your IP address, stop
tracking by websites, or protect you from malware. For stronger privacy, look
into a reputable VPN later — but start with the basics above.

### Private browsing checklist

- [ ] Know how to open a private window in your browser (Ctrl+Shift+N / Cmd+Shift+N in most browsers)
- [ ] Use private windows on shared or public devices
- [ ] Close private windows when finished
- [ ] Don't rely on private browsing to hide your activity from websites or your network
- [ ] Clear downloads separately — private browsing may keep downloaded files

## 5. Phone and email hygiene

### Phone hygiene

Your phone number is tied to many accounts. Protecting it reduces spam and
sIM-swap risk.

- **Don't post your number publicly.** Social media, resumes, and public
  profiles are scraped by bots.
- **Use a secondary number for sign-ups.** Free apps like Google Voice give you
  a number you can use for shopping, newsletters, and accounts — keeping your
  real number off marketing lists.
- **Don't answer unknown calls.** Let them go to voicemail. Scammers spoof
  real-looking numbers.
- **Set a PIN on your phone carrier account.** Call your carrier and ask to add
  a port/PIN lock so nobody can move your number to another phone without it.

### Email hygiene

Your inbox is the gateway to most of your accounts.

- **Use separate emails for different purposes.** One for personal/family, one
  for shopping/newsletters, one for banking. Keeps things organized and limits
  fallout if one address gets spam.
- **Don't click links in unexpected emails.** Hover to see where a link goes.
  When in doubt, go to the company's website directly instead of clicking.
- **Unsubscribe from mailing lists you don't read.** Less inbox clutter means
  fewer phishing emails can hide.
- **Turn on spam filtering.** Most providers do this by default; check that it's
  on.

### Phone/email checklist

- [ ] Don't post your personal phone number on public profiles or social media
- [ ] Consider a secondary number (e.g., Google Voice) for sign-ups and shopping
- [ ] Set a port/PIN lock with your phone carrier
- [ ] Use a separate email for shopping/newsletters vs. personal/banking
- [ ] Don't click links in unexpected emails; visit sites directly instead
- [ ] Unsubscribe from mailing lists you no longer read

## Putting it together

You don't have to do everything today. Pick one section, finish its checklist,
then move on when you're ready. Small, steady steps add up.

Suggested order:
1. Passwords (password manager + unique passwords)
2. Two-factor authentication (email first, then carrier and bank)
3. Phone and email hygiene (carrier PIN, secondary number, separate emails)
4. Data-broker opt-outs (bigger time investment, do after the basics)
5. Private browsing (use when it fits — shared devices, one-off searches)
